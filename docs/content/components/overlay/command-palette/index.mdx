The `<CommandPalette>` component opens a search field over a list of pages and actions. People press <kbd>⌘</kbd>+<kbd>K</kbd> (<kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>K</kbd> on Windows and Linux) from anywhere in the app, type a few letters and press <kbd>Enter</kbd> to go to the top match. It suits power users who live in the keyboard and apps with more pages than a sidebar can show comfortably.

## Anatomy

A command palette consists of an optional button and the palette itself, which opens on an underlay. The palette holds a search field, the list of results grouped under titles, and a legend of the keys. When nothing matches or results are still loading, a message takes the place of the list. On small screens the palette opens as a tray from the bottom edge.

<CommandPaletteAnatomy />

- **Button:** An optional button that looks like a search field, reads "Go to…" and shows the shortcut. The shortcut works without it.
- **Search field:** The header of the palette. It gets focus when the palette opens and shows a clear button once there is a query, or a spinner while results load.
- **Group:** An optional title over related items, for example Pages and Actions.
- **Item:** A single page or action, with an optional icon and description.
- **Active row:** The highlighted row, which <kbd>Enter</kbd> runs. There is always one while there are results.
- **List:** Holds the groups and items and scrolls when they don't fit.
- **Legend:** A footer listing the keys to navigate, select and close, and the prefixes that narrow the search to one group. It is built in and hidden on touch devices.
- **Underlay:** Dims the page behind the palette. Clicking it closes the palette.

## Usage

As an app grows, the way to a page gets longer. Pages end up nested in the navigation, frequent actions sit several clicks deep, and opening a known event or order means going to a list, filtering and scanning. A command palette gives people one place they can reach from anywhere: they type what they want and land there.

Navigation is the core of it. People open the palette, type a few letters of a page name and go to that page without remembering where it sits in the sidebar. Two more kinds of content fit the same habit:

- **Actions** people start often, such as "Create event". They skip the detour to the page where the button lives.
- **Records** people know by name or number, such as an event or an order. They skip the list page, the filter and the scanning.

A palette pays off in apps with more pages than the sidebar shows comfortably and for people who work in the app every day. In an app with a handful of pages the sidebar already shows everything at a glance, and the palette is one more thing to learn. It complements the navigation and never replaces it. People who don't know the shortcut, or who use a touch device, still need the sidebar and the buttons on the page, so don't make the palette the only way to reach a page or an action.

### Content

Name items by what people look for and use the same words as the sidebar and the page titles. Start actions with a verb, so "Create event" reads apart from the "Events" page.

Group the results, for example into Pages, Actions and Events, and keep the groups and their order the same every time. People learn where things are and type less over time. Records usually come from the server, see [Searching many records](#searching-many-records).

Give a page the same icon it has in the navigation, so people recognise it in both places. Records and actions get one icon for their kind, for example a ticket for every event, not one per record. Within a group, give every item an icon or none, as in the [Menu](/components/overlay/menu). The label carries the meaning and the icon only helps people scan.

Some actions also have their own keyboard shortcut. People who run such an action every day are faster with the shortcut than with the palette, but most never find out it exists. Show the shortcut on the row with [`<Keyboard>`](/components/content/keyboard), so they pick it up while they use the palette.

Records often share a name. Add a short description that tells them apart, like the event number and date. When people search for something the label doesn't show, add it to `textValue`, which the search matches against. In the demo above, typing "16" finds "Comedy Slam".

<Callout title="Avoid destructive actions" type="warning">
  Leave out destructive actions, like deleting a record. The first match is
  always active, so a quick <kbd>Enter</kbd> can run something people didn't
  mean to. If one belongs in the palette, open a confirmation dialog instead of
  running it right away.
</Callout>

### Shortcut

The shortcut works on every page, so render one palette per app, inside the app shell. A second palette with the same shortcut would compete for the key. If a page needs its own palette, pass `shortcut={false}` to its trigger.

The button is optional, but it tells people the palette exists and shows the shortcut. The [App frame](/patterns/layout/app-frame) places it in the middle of the top navigation, as in the demo above. Any other button inside `<CommandPalette.Trigger>` opens the palette too, for example an icon button where space is tight.

The shortcut also fires inside text fields, as it does in GitHub, Linear and Slack. If another handler already used the key, for example a rich-text editor that inserts a link on <kbd>⌘</kbd>+<kbd>K</kbd>, the palette leaves it alone. Change the key with `shortcut="j"` on the trigger, or turn it off with `shortcut={false}` when your app already uses it.

### Before people type

An empty query is the most common start, and the full list is the least helpful answer to it. Show what people most likely want next, in this order:

- **Favourites:** pages and records people marked themselves. They come first, because people chose them.
- **Recent:** the records people opened last, newest first.
- **Frequent actions:** the few actions people start most, such as "Create event".

Keep each group to about five rows, so everything fits without scrolling. Show an item only once: a favourite that was also opened recently stays under Favourites. Leave out the full list of pages, the sidebar already shows it, and anything people didn't ask for, like news or tips. Once people type, switch to the regular groups.

Control the query to know when it is empty. Favourites and recent records come from your app, ideally from the server, so they follow people across devices.

### Searching many records

People often know exactly which record they need, an event by its name or an order by its number. Without the palette they open the list page, filter it and scan the rows to get there. Searching records from the palette turns this into a few keystrokes, from any page.

#### What to show

- **Match what people know:** search the fields people remember, such as the name and the number of an event, or the order number and the customer's name. Put an exact number match first, people who type a number mean that record.
- **Show a few per kind:** about three to five records per group, and no more than about ten rows in total. Beyond that, people stop reading the suggestions and start scanning. Once a prefix leaves only one kind, the group has the list to itself and can show more, about eight. Show what tells the records apart in the description, like the date of an event.
- **Offer more in two steps:** in the mixed list, end a group that has more matches with "More events…". Before people type, the few recent records are only a sample, so offer the row there too. Otherwise people take the recent records for everything there is. Set `more` on the group for it: the row narrows the search to that group and keeps the term, so people get more of one kind without knowing its prefix. In the narrowed list, end the group with "Show all events for “jazz”", which opens the list page with the query already in its search. The palette is for jumping to one record, the list page is for browsing, sorting and comparing many.
- **Keep the rest fast:** pages and actions show right away, only the records group waits for the server. Results that arrive after people have finished typing don't help them anymore, so keep the search quick and the number of results small.

#### Narrowing to one kind

In a mixed list, records compete with pages and actions, and record search stays invisible until people type a name. For each kind of record people look up often, give its group a short prefix, like `e:` for events and `v:` for venues. A query that starts with the prefix narrows the search to that group: `e:jazz` shows only events that match "jazz".

The footer lists the prefixes next to the keys, like "e: Events", so people come across them every time they open the palette. Screen readers hear each prefix as part of its group's name. A prefix alone should list the first records of that kind, not only the recent ones, so people don't take a short list for everything there is. Keep the prefixes few and memorable, one letter per kind of record.

### When nothing matches

Say what people can search for, so they know whether to rephrase or look elsewhere, for example "No page with that name. Try “events” or “orders”." Don't blame the query. Pass the message as `emptyState`.

If your app has a full search, end the list with a row that runs it for the query. The list is then never empty, and <kbd>Enter</kbd> always leads somewhere.

### Palette or search field

The palette and a [search field](/components/form/search-field) can sit in the same place in the top navigation, but they solve different problems. The palette takes people somewhere: to a page, a record or an action, from anywhere in the app. A search field narrows what is already on the page, for example the rows of a table. Use a search field when people look through results together, compare them or refine a filter, and the palette when they know what they want and need to get there fast.

## Accessibility

The palette follows the [combobox pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/combobox/). Focus stays in the search field while the arrow keys move a virtual focus through the list, so screen readers announce the active row and people can keep typing. The empty and loading states sit inside the list, where screen readers announce them.

| Key                                                      | Action                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| <kbd>⌘</kbd>+<kbd>K</kbd> / <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>K</kbd> | Opens the palette from anywhere, also inside text fields. |
| <kbd>↑</kbd> / <kbd>↓</kbd>                              | Moves the active row.                                     |
| <kbd>Enter</kbd>                                         | Runs the active row.                                      |
| <kbd>Escape</kbd>                                        | Clears the search, then closes the palette.               |

Letters typed into the search field stay in the palette. Shortcuts your app binds to single keys, for example "c" to create, don't fire while people type a query.

The search field is the only Tab stop. The caret is its focus indicator, so the field has no extra ring. When the palette closes, focus returns to where it was.
